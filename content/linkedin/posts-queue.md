## [ ] Day 1 — The BOJ hiked and the yen fell

--- POST ---
The Bank of Japan raised interest rates this week to the highest level since 1995.

The yen promptly fell.

If that sounds backwards, it is worth knowing why it happened, because the same logic runs through every currency including the rupee.

Three things did it:

1. The hike was already priced. Traders bought yen weeks ago. By decision day there was nothing left to buy.

2. Two of the nine board members voted against it. A hike passed 7-2 tells you the committee may struggle to deliver the next one.

3. The governor sounded cautious in the press conference.

Currencies do not trade the decision. They trade the path.

This is also why the rupee weakened past 96 in the same week the RBI did absolutely nothing. Our side of the rate gap was still. The American side was moving.

If you only remember one line from this: the announcement is the least informative part of a central bank meeting. The vote split and the guidance carry the information.

Not investment advice.

## [ ] Day 2 — Onions did what oil could not

--- POST ---
India's inflation print this month had a detail almost nobody led with.

Retail inflation: 4.82%, a seven-month high.

Everyone expected oil to be the culprit. Brent had crossed $100 for the first time since July.

It wasn't oil.

Food inflation came in at 5.95%. Inside that:
• Onion: +48%
• Ginger: +74%
• Garlic: +44%

Rural inflation ran at 5.23% against 4.31% in cities, because food is a bigger share of a rural household's basket.

Why this matters beyond the kitchen:

Rural demand recovery has been the single most repeated phrase on Indian earnings calls this year. This print works against it. Two-wheelers, entry-level consumer goods, mass FMCG all depend on the exact budget that vegetables just squeezed.

And the policy problem is worse than the number looks. A central bank can look through an oil shock. It is much harder to look through the second supply shock stacked on top of the first, because that is when households stop treating price rises as temporary.

Rate cuts do not grow onions.

## [ ] Day 3 — Applying for more IPO lots does not help

--- POST ---
The most expensive misunderstanding in Indian IPO investing:

"I'll apply for 5 lots instead of 1, so I have a better chance of allotment."

You don't. Here is the actual mechanism.

When the retail portion of an IPO is oversubscribed, SEBI's framework tries to give every applicant at least ONE lot. If there aren't enough shares for that, the registrar runs a computerised lottery at the single-lot level.

So in a 4x oversubscribed retail book:
• A 1-lot applicant has roughly a 1-in-4 chance of one lot
• A 5-lot applicant has roughly a 1-in-4 chance of one lot
• Nobody gets five

Bid size only matters when the retail portion is UNDER-subscribed. Then everyone gets what they asked for, and size is the only thing that decides your holding.

The one legitimate lever: one application per PAN. A household with three adults holding separate PANs and demat accounts has three entries. Splitting one person's application across accounts is not the same thing, and duplicates against a single PAN can get all of them rejected.

Also worth knowing: your money is blocked, never debited, under ASBA and UPI. If you aren't allotted, the block simply releases.

Not investment advice.

## [ ] Day 4 — The number under everything

--- POST ---
One number explains most of what Indian markets have done this quarter, and it isn't the Nifty.

The US 10-year Treasury yield hit about 5.04%. Highest since 2007.

That yield is the closest thing global finance has to a price of safety. When it rises, every riskier asset on earth has to justify itself against a higher bar. Including ours.

How it reaches India, in order of speed:

→ Flows. Foreign investors have sold roughly ₹2.37 lakh crore of Indian equities in 2026.
→ Currency. The rupee touched 96 per dollar before closing at 95.94.
→ Corporate borrowing. Indian companies raising money abroad pay more.
→ Valuations. A higher discount rate compresses the present value of far-off earnings, which is why expensive growth names feel it before cyclicals.
→ Policy space. A wider rate gap makes RBI easing harder to justify.

The part worth internalising:

A rising risk-free rate does not make Indian companies worse. It makes the alternative to owning them better.

And the most expensive assumption available right now is that 5% is permanent. That call has been wrong in both directions repeatedly over the past decade.

Not investment advice.

## [ ] Day 5 — Read the anchor book before you bid

--- POST ---
Retail investors get one number before an IPO opens: the price band.

It tells you nothing about whether anyone credible thinks that price is fair.

The anchor book does, and almost nobody reads it.

Anchor investors are institutions allotted shares one working day before the issue opens, at a price inside the band, with a minimum application of ₹10 crore. The full list of who bid and how much is published before retail bidding starts.

Three questions it answers:

1. Did institutions accept the price? Anchors bid inside the band. A book filled at the top is a different statement from one that needed the floor.

2. Who showed up? Domestic mutual funds and long-only global funds hold for years. Some multi-strategy names are structurally shorter term. The mix tells you what your shareholder base looks like in month two.

3. How concentrated is it? Three names taking most of the book becomes concentrated selling pressure at the same moment.

And the part with an expiry date: 50% of anchor shares unlock after 30 days, the rest after 90.

A strong anchor book proves institutions were willing to fund the issue. It does not prove the price is right. Those are different sentences.

Not investment advice.

## [ ] Day 6 — India now has two closed sea gates

--- POST ---
For six months this was a one-chokepoint crisis. It is now a two-chokepoint crisis, and the second one hits the other side of India's balance sheet.

The Strait of Hormuz controls the oil India BUYS.
Bab el-Mandeb controls the goods India SELLS.

Both are contested at once.

The asymmetry matters. An oil chokepoint raises what India pays. A shipping chokepoint reduces what India earns. Until this month, only the first was in play.

What the detour costs:
• Mumbai to Rotterdam: ~15,700 km via Suez, ~23,000 km around the Cape
• 10 to 15 extra days each way
• Tanker fuel on a Gulf-Europe voyage: $1.26m → $2.87m
• Asia-Europe container rates running 25-40% above pre-crisis

And the cost nobody puts in the freight table:

Two extra weeks at sea is two extra weeks before an exporter gets paid. For a mid-sized Indian exporter, the working capital hit plus war-risk insurance often exceeds the freight increase itself.

Indian refiners sit on both lists at once. They buy crude that comes through one contested strait and sell diesel that leaves through the other.

Not investment advice.

## [ ] Day 7 — A $664 billion order book and negative cash flow

--- POST ---
Oracle just reported the largest order book in enterprise software history.

It also burned $23.7 billion of cash getting there.

Both numbers are true, and they are the same story.

The quarter: revenue $19.3bn, up 30%. Cloud infrastructure up 121% to $7.4bn. Remaining performance obligations at $664bn, up $209bn in a year.

The other side: fiscal 2026 free cash flow was NEGATIVE $23.7bn, because record operating cash flow of ~$32bn was entirely consumed by data centre construction. Capex was $55.7bn in one quarter. About $70bn is guided for FY27.

The market couldn't decide either. The stock fell 5.4% in the regular session, then rose 4.3% after hours on the same set of numbers.

Here is the tension in one line:

A backlog is a promise about the future. Capital expenditure is a bill in the present.

What makes it hard to judge from outside: a reported backlog tells you the size of the commitments and nothing about their durability. If AI demand cools, contracts get renegotiated or delayed long before they become revenue, while the data centres built to serve them stay on the balance sheet.

That asymmetry is why a record backlog and a record cash burn can be the same story.

Not investment advice.

## [ ] Day 8 — The AI trade moved from chips to cooling pipes

--- POST ---
Two deals this month that almost nobody framed together:

→ Blackstone agreed to buy a maker of data centre liquid cooling components.
→ Flex agreed to pay about $4.4 billion for EPC Power, which makes power conversion equipment.

Neither target designs a processor. Both sell the physical layer that decides how much compute a building can actually run.

That is the story. The AI constraint has moved from chips to heat and electricity.

Why cooling became investable: AI racks concentrate far more heat in the same floor area than the last hardware generation. Past a point, moving air cannot remove it fast enough. Cooling stopped being a facilities line item and became a limit on how much hardware a site can hold.

Why power conversion did: large campuses are moving to 800V distribution to carry more power with lower losses, and grid-forming equipment is what lets very large, very variable loads connect to systems never designed for them.

The tell on how serious this is: Flex plans to spin its Cloud and Power Infrastructure segment into a separately listed company in Q1 2027.

For India, the read is direct. The domestic data centre build needs the same cooling systems, power conversion gear, transformers and switchgear that global hyperscalers are now buying. Equipment makers face demand they haven't seen. Developers face longer lead times and firmer prices.

Every infrastructure cycle ends the same way: the first money goes into the thing everyone can name, the later money goes into whatever turns out to be scarce.

Not investment advice.

## [ ] Day 9 — India makes a quarter of the world's iPhones

--- POST ---
Apple launched the iPhone 18 Pro this month. The interesting part wasn't the camera.

Full-scale production of the Pro and Pro Max is already running in India, for the US and European markets.

The numbers behind that shift:

• India is on track for ~26% of global iPhone production in 2026, up from ~6% four years ago
• iPhone exports from India hit a record ₹2 trillion in FY26, up about 33%
• That makes the iPhone India's single largest branded export across 5,000+ product categories
• India's total smartphone exports were ~₹2.6 trillion, and iPhones alone were more than 75% of it

And a quiet reversal: Tata Electronics overtook Foxconn on exports across the five-year PLI period, $26.3bn against $25.6bn.

The policy engine also changed. PLI ended on 31 March 2026, having delivered ₹11.61 lakh crore of mobile production against an ₹8.12 lakh crore target. Its replacement, MPMS, runs FY27-31 with ₹62,500 crore, and it pays extra for domestic component sourcing and for design and R&D.

That difference is the whole point. PLI rewarded assembly volume. The new scheme pays for the parts of the value chain India does not yet own.

Four years ago the question was whether Apple would make phones in India at all. The question now is whether India ends up owning what's inside them.

## [ ] Day 10 — Your EMI does not move on RBI day

--- POST ---
Every RBI decision ends with the same headline: "here's what it means for your EMI."

For most borrowers the honest answer is: nothing, until a date printed in your loan agreement arrives.

How a floating home loan actually works in India:

For loans sanctioned after 1 October 2019, banks must link the rate to an external benchmark, and most use the RBI repo rate. Your rate = repo + a spread set at sanction.

That loan re-prices at its RESET date, which must fall at least once every three months. Depending where you sit in that cycle, the gap between a policy change and a change in your outflow runs from days to ~90.

Older loans on MCLR re-price on the bank's own cost-of-funds clock, typically every 6 or 12 months. Same policy decision, months apart.

Two things most borrowers have never checked:

1. Your spread. It was fixed at sanction and generally stays with the loan. Two customers of the same bank on the same benchmark can pay different rates for years.

2. What your bank adjusts. Many default to changing the TENURE, not the EMI. A rate rise can quietly add months to your loan while your monthly outflow looks unchanged. You can usually ask for the opposite.

For scale: the 125 bps of cuts through 2025 was worth roughly ₹3,900 a month on a ₹50 lakh, 20-year loan.

Three things to look up today: your benchmark, your spread, your reset date. All three are in the sanction letter.

## [ ] Day 11 — When the premium brand and the value brand slow together

--- POST ---
Two American companies that share almost nothing told the market the same thing this month.

Lululemon fell about 18% after cutting its full-year guidance for the THIRD time this year. Q2 revenue down 4%, comparable sales down 9%.

McDonald's hit a 52-week low near $256, about 18% below its high, on weaker spending by lower-income customers.

One sells ₹8,000 leggings. The other sells value meals.

When premium discretionary and low-cost fast food weaken in the same quarter, the explanation that fits both is the household budget, not the brand. Nike fell ~10% and On Holding ~14% over a comparable stretch, which makes the coincidence harder to argue away.

The India question this raises is not about American shoppers.

India's own CPI came in at 4.82% with food at 5.95%, and rural inflation ABOVE urban. Rural households spend more of their income on food, so a vegetable-led shock compresses exactly the discretionary budget that two-wheelers, entry-level durables and mass-market apparel depend on.

The festive cycle from Navratri to Diwali is when many Indian consumer companies book a disproportionate share of annual sales. That makes the next six weeks an unusually clean read on whether Indian household demand is holding up better than the American version.

One thing worth watching in any result: traffic, not revenue. A chain can hold revenue by raising prices while serving fewer people. That mix is fragile.

## [ ] Day 12 — Crypto's biggest bill died on a procedural vote

--- POST ---
The most consequential crypto vote in years ended in the most ordinary way possible: a procedural motion about whether to start debating it at all.

The US Senate failed to advance the CLARITY Act. More than 40 senators voted against the motion to proceed, short of the 60 required.

What the bill would have settled, and now doesn't:
• A clear split of authority between the SEC and the CFTC
• A statutory test for when a token is a commodity rather than a security
• An explicit classification in law for Bitcoin and Ethereum

Bitcoin fell to about $76,300 on the day. Prediction markets had already cut the odds of it becoming law in 2026 from ~30% to ~14% before the vote, which is why the move was a slide rather than a collapse.

The part that gets underrated:

Nothing became illegal. The failure preserves the status quo, which is regulation by enforcement rather than by statute. That is workable for large exchanges with legal departments and punishing for smaller issuers.

So the cost is not a crash. It is a drag. Every quarter without statutory clarity is a quarter where some allocator defers, some product doesn't launch, and some builder incorporates offshore.

The US now has a statute for stablecoins and none for market structure.

For Indian investors, nothing changes on tax: 30% plus cess, no loss set-off, 1% TDS.

Not investment advice.

## [ ] Day 13 — How to read a dot plot without overreacting

--- POST ---
Four times a year, a chart of anonymous dots moves more money than most data releases. Most people reacting to it have never read the footnote saying it is not a plan.

The Fed's dot plot: each meeting participant marks where they think the policy rate should sit at the end of this year and the next few. One dot each. No names.

Three readings sit inside it, and most commentary uses only the first.

1. The MEDIAN dot. The headline number. Where the typical participant expects rates to end up.

2. The SPREAD. The distance between highest and lowest. A tight cluster means a predictable committee. A wide scatter means the next few decisions could go either way, which a median hides completely.

3. The SHIFT from the last release. Usually what actually gets traded, because a median moving from one hike to two changes the expected path even when the current decision was fully anticipated.

Worth holding onto: these are conditional forecasts by individuals, not a committee commitment. The June 2026 chart reportedly had nine participants expecting at least one hike, eight expecting none, and one still expecting a cut. That is not a settled direction, it is a coin flip with a median printed on top.

How to watch a release without whiplash: compare new median to old, then the spread, then the statement, then the press conference. Markets frequently move one way on the dots and reverse 30 minutes later on what the chair says about them.

Not investment advice.

## [ ] Day 14 — ₹1.10 lakh crore, and 98% of it stays in India

--- POST ---
India's Defence Acquisition Council cleared procurement proposals worth about ₹1.10 lakh crore ($11.64bn) this month.

The number that matters is not the headline. It's this: roughly 98% of that value is to be sourced from Indian companies.

The list is deliberately unglamorous. CBRN reconnaissance vehicles, high mobility vehicles, advanced light helicopters, trawl tanks, surveillance radars, warship propulsion, electronic warfare equipment. Army, Navy and Air Force.

That is capability filling, not headline platform buying, and it is the kind of spending that lands on domestic order books rather than foreign primes.

Context on the books it lands on:
• HAL: order book ~₹2.54 lakh crore (March 2026), roughly 7-8 years of revenue visibility
• BEL: ~₹73,882 crore (April 2026), about 2.5-3 years

The caveat most coverage skipped:

A DAC clearance is an Acceptance of Necessity. It permits tendering to begin. No vendor, no final price, no delivery date. The gap between clearance and signed contract has historically run into years, and some of it never converts.

So the number to track for earnings is contracts signed in FY27, not proposals cleared in September 2026.

What's genuinely new here is that a package this size could be written with 98% domestic content at all. That sentence was not available in 2016.

Not investment advice.
