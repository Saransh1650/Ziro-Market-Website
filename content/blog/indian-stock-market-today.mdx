The week ended better than it traded, which is the only good thing about it. **The Nifty 50 closed at 23,270.60 on 17 September 2026, up 0.23%, while the Sensex slipped 0.03% to 74,314.59, and both opened higher again on 18 September, with the Nifty near 23,316 and the Sensex near 74,474 in early trade.** Realty, pharma, auto and metals led the recovery, with banking shares the drag.

The reversal is the part worth reading. **The Sensex opened at 75,369.63 and the Nifty at 23,576.15 on early buying in technology stocks and HDFC Bank, then gave it all back as crude and bond yields took over.** Financials fell about 1.8% and autos about 2%, while HCLTech, Infosys and TCS cushioned the fall.

Neither cause is Indian. **Brent crude returned to about $107 a barrel after the Oman talks on Hormuz shipping were postponed, and the US 10-year Treasury yield touched 5%**, which together set the cost of everything India imports and the price of the money that funds its markets.

## What moved the market

Oil did most of it. **Brent has risen about 19.3% in a month, settling near $104 on 11 September 2026 after touching $106.11, after Houthi strikes on Saudi energy facilities added to the Strait of Hormuz disruption and Saudi crude output fell by roughly 1.9 million barrels a day in August.** India imports more than 90% of the crude it uses, so this is an input cost for the entire index, as our [crude oil price today](/blog/crude-oil-price-today) page tracks.

The second driver arrived from Washington. **US CPI rose 0.4% in August for an annual rate of 3.4%, with gasoline alone accounting for over a third of the monthly increase, and market-implied odds of a Federal Reserve hike on 16 September jumped to about 90%.** The detail is in our [US CPI August 2026](/blog/us-cpi-august-2026-fed-hike-india) piece.

The currency absorbed both. **The rupee fell to a six-week low past 96**, tracked on our [rupee versus dollar today](/blog/rupee-dollar-today) page.

## Who is buying and who is selling

The split has not changed all year. **Foreign institutional investors sold Rs 438.24 crore of Indian equities on 10 September, taking September sales to about Rs 12,912 crore and the 2026 total to roughly Rs 2,37,353 crore**, a scale our [FPI outflows](/blog/fpi-outflows-india-2026-record) piece covers.

**Domestic institutions have been the counterparty for most of 2026, funded by record monthly SIP flows**, which is why an index carrying record foreign selling is down roughly 11% from its 26,373.20 record close of 5 January rather than far more. The mechanics are in [how to read FII and DII activity](/blog/how-to-read-fii-dii-activity).

## What comes next

**The one piece of hoped-for good news did not survive the weekend.** Oman postponed the 14 September Salalah meeting between Iran, Gulf states and Iraq after Saudi objections and a Bahraini refusal to attend, and Brent went straight back to about $107, covered in our [Hormuz talks](/blog/hormuz-talks-oman-september-2026-oil-india) piece.

**India's August CPI came in at 4.82% on 14 September, a seven-month high, with food inflation at 5.95%**, which effectively shuts the Reserve Bank of India's cut window for 2026, as our [India CPI August 2026](/blog/india-cpi-august-2026-inflation-rbi) piece sets out.

**The Federal Reserve raised rates to 3.75% to 4.00% on 16 September, its first increase since 2023, and projected about 4.1% through 2027**, covered in our [Fed rate hike](/blog/fed-rate-hike-september-2026-india-impact) analysis.

**The NSE IPO opened on 17 September at a price band of Rs 1,700 to Rs 1,785 and was about 0.42 times subscribed on day one**, a Rs 22,569 crore offer for sale that will absorb investor capital during the same week, covered in our [NSE IPO](/blog/nse-ipo-drhp-2026-india-largest-ever) analysis. The full diary is in our [September market calendar](/blog/market-events-calendar-september-2026).

## Risks to monitor

<Callout type="risk" title="Two shocks, one direction">

Expensive oil and a hawkish Federal Reserve hit the rupee from opposite ends, one through the import bill and one through capital flows, and the RBI cannot ease into that combination without adding currency pressure. That is how a global energy shock becomes an Indian interest rate problem.

</Callout>

The second risk is the reverse. **A ceasefire in the Gulf would take the war premium out of oil quickly**, and the same index that fell on the shock would recover fastest on its resolution.

The third is earnings. **The next quarterly season begins in October, and margin pressure from fuel and freight costs shows up there rather than in this week's price action.** This is general information, not investment advice.

A market that falls 2% in a week on entirely foreign causes is not a verdict on Indian companies. It is a reminder of how much of an Indian portfolio is priced off a tanker route and a room in Washington.
