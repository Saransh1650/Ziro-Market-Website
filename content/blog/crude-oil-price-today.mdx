The oil market's brief calm is over, and this time the move is bigger. **Crude oil price today is about $80 a barrel for WTI and above $86 for Brent as of July 14, 2026, surging as the US bombs Iran for a third consecutive day and Iran attacks tankers near the Strait of Hormuz, reviving fears of a major supply shock.** From near $76 just days earlier, Brent has jumped more than $10.

For India, which imports most of its oil, the timing could hardly be worse. The surge lands just as inflation has topped 4% and the rupee has cracked past 96 to a record low.

## What Is Happening

The escalation has been relentless. **The US has bombed Iran for three consecutive days, and Iran's Revolutionary Guards have attacked at least two more tankers near the Strait of Hormuz**, the chokepoint that carries roughly a fifth of the world's oil. Each strike and counter-strike adds to the fear that supply through the Strait could be cut, and traders are pricing that risk aggressively.

The price response has been sharp. Brent jumped about 4.3% to above $86 and WTI rose toward $80, extending a move that has taken crude from near $76 just days ago. **Shipowners are increasingly reluctant to send tankers through a live conflict zone, which tightens supply expectations regardless of what is actually pumped.**

This is the third leg of the 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis, and the most severe. After an earlier spike, a pullback as markets hoped for calm, oil has now surged again as the fighting intensified, showing how quickly the situation can swing.

## Why This Matters for India

Few countries lose more from costlier oil than India. **India imports more than 85% of its crude, so Brent above $85 sharply widens the import bill, pressures the trade deficit, and weakens the rupee**, which has slid past 96 to the dollar to a record low. The oil surge is a direct hit to the economy's most sensitive pressure points.

The inflation channel is already showing. June CPI rose to 4.38%, above the RBI's 4% target, partly on higher fuel costs, as covered in our [India June CPI](/blog/india-june-cpi-inflation-2026) piece, and a sustained oil spike would keep that pressure building. Our [how crude oil affects the Indian economy](/blog/how-crude-oil-affects-indian-economy) explainer traces the full chain from the barrel to the budget.

For the market, the surge dragged Indian equities lower, with oil-sensitive and rate-sensitive sectors hit hardest, as detailed in our [Indian stock market today](/blog/indian-stock-market-today) wrap. Upstream producers benefit from higher crude, but the broader economy pays the price.

## What To Watch

The first thing to watch is the Strait of Hormuz. **Because the rally is built on the threat to shipping, any actual blockage or further tanker attacks could send prices spiking well beyond $86**, while a genuine de-escalation could pull them back fast.

The second is the scale of the US-Iran conflict. Repeated strikes raise the risk of a wider war, and how far the fighting spreads will shape how large a risk premium the market demands.

The third is the demand and supply response. Whether OPEC+ raises output to calm prices, and whether high prices start to dent demand, will influence where crude settles once the immediate shock is priced.

## Risks To Monitor

The clearest risk is a genuine disruption to Hormuz. A prolonged closure would be a supply shock capable of pushing oil far higher and hitting import-dependent economies like India hard.

A second risk is a wider regional war. The conflict has already drawn in strikes across the Gulf, and further spread would keep a large premium in oil.

The third, for India specifically, is the compounding hit of costly oil, a record-low rupee, and rising inflation, which together squeeze growth and limit the RBI's options. This is general information, not investment advice.

Brent above $86 is the clearest sign yet that the Gulf conflict, not supply and demand, is driving the oil market. For an importer like India, the surge threatens the rupee, inflation, and growth at once, which is why the Strait of Hormuz remains the single most important chart for the economy right now.
