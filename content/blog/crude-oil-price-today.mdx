The oil shock has taken its first real step back. **Brent crude traded at about $99.29 a barrel on 19 September 2026, up 1.18% on the day but down from roughly $104 the session before and about $107 in mid-September, the first time the benchmark has held under $100 since the Strait of Hormuz squeeze tightened.** It is still up about 8% from $92.06 on 24 August.

For an economy whose crude import dependence crossed 90% in FY26, this is not an energy story. It is an inflation story, a rupee story and an interest rate story arriving together, and the full transmission is set out in our note on [Brent crossing $100](/blog/brent-crude-100-dollars-september-2026).

**The war now has a second chokepoint.** Houthi forces seized the Red Sea port of Mocha and then the length of Yemen's Red Sea coastline in September 2026, putting Bab el-Mandeb, the southern gate to the Suez Canal, under the same cloud as Hormuz. India's crude comes in through one and its exports to Europe go out through the other, a squeeze our [two chokepoints](/blog/two-chokepoints-hormuz-bab-el-mandeb-india-2026) piece sets out.

## Why did strikes move the price when sanctions did not?

Because one threatens permission and the other threatens supply. **Sanctions restrict who may legally buy Iranian oil, which redirects barrels at a discount; air strikes near the Strait of Hormuz put the physical movement of about a fifth of the world's seaborne oil at risk**, and the market prices those two things very differently.

The knock-on effect landed somewhere unexpected. **Higher oil raised expected inflation, which helped push the Federal Reserve to raise rates to 3.75% to 4.00% on 16 September 2026, and higher rates are normally what caps gold**, though gold rose to about $4,383 an ounce on 18 September regardless, the mechanism our [why gold fell when the war restarted](/blog/why-gold-fell-war-restarted-2026) piece explains.

## How did the price get here?

The rally was built in stages. **A US authorisation permitting Iranian crude sales expired on 21 August 2026 without an extension, Operation Economic Outcast sanctions landed on 24 August, and US and Iranian forces then began trading strikes at sea through early September**, which took Brent from $92.06 on 24 August to above $100 on 9 September and to about $106.11 intraday on 11 September. Our [Iran sanctions and India](/blog/india-iran-oil-sanctions-august-2026) analysis covers the sanctions step.

## What it means for India

The transmission is direct and fast. **Every sustained $10 on the barrel widens India's import bill, costs an estimated 20 to 30 basis points of GDP growth, and pushes up transport and fuel costs that reach food and manufactured goods within a quarter.** The rupee fell to a six-week low past 96 on 15 September 2026, with the Reserve Bank of India selling dollars to slow it.

The inflation link is the one that matters for policy. **Indian retail inflation rose to 4.82% in August 2026, a seven-month high, and fuel is one of the components that lifts everything else**, which narrows the space the Reserve Bank of India has to cut from its 5.25% repo rate. Our [will the RBI cut rates again in 2026](/blog/will-rbi-cut-rates-2026) outlook sets out how tight that calculation has become.

There is a second, sharper edge for India this time, and it arrived on 18 September. **President Trump signed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act into law, authorising tariffs of up to 100% on the five largest buyers of Russian energy and directing action within 30 days.** India imported about 2.08 million barrels a day of Russian crude in August 2026, roughly 45% of its total, which places it squarely inside the law's scope.

Equities have registered both sides of it. The Nifty 50 closed at 23,346.40 on 18 September 2026, up 0.33% on the day as oil eased, but still down for a sixth straight week, as our [Indian stock market today](/blog/indian-stock-market-today) wrap covers.

## What to watch

**The first thing is enforcement rather than announcement.** The package named Chinese refineries but stopped short of major Chinese financial institutions, and China buys more than 80% of Iran's shipped oil, so whether sanctions reach those banks decides whether cargoes actually strand.

**The second is whether the new tariff authority on buyers of Russian energy is actually used inside its 30-day window**, since applying it to India or China would be an inflation event in its own right, separate from anything happening at Hormuz. The Oman-brokered Salalah meeting remains postponed with no new date, covered in our [Hormuz talks](/blog/hormuz-talks-oman-september-2026-oil-india) piece.

**The third is the Strait of Hormuz itself, which carries about a fifth of the world's seaborne oil.** Sanctions restrict who may buy; a closure would restrict whether anyone can, and the difference between those two is worth roughly the entire current risk premium, as our [what happens if the Strait of Hormuz closes](/blog/what-happens-if-strait-of-hormuz-closes) explainer sets out.

## Risks to monitor

<Callout type="risk" title="The combination India cannot hedge">

Expensive oil and a hawkish US Federal Reserve are the same trade hitting the rupee from two directions. Oil widens the trade deficit while high US yields pull capital out, and the Reserve Bank of India cannot ease into that without risking the currency. That is the scenario in which a global energy shock becomes an Indian interest rate problem.

</Callout>

The second risk runs the other way. Oil near $99 still prices a great deal of bad news, so a ceasefire or a negotiated de-escalation could take another ten dollars out quickly.

The third is OPEC+. Higher prices give the group room to raise output, which would cap the rally, while any decision to hold output steady into a supply squeeze would extend it. This is general information, not investment advice.

Brent under $100 buys India room it did not have a week ago, and a law signed the same week could take it straight back. What happens next will not be announced at a press conference. It shows up in tanker manifests, insurance quotes and bank compliance decisions, and only then in the price of Indian petrol.
