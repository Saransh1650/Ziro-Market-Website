Every evening after the market closes, two numbers get quoted more than almost any other: how much foreign investors bought or sold, and how much domestic institutions did. **That single line, FII versus DII activity, is the market's clearest window into who is actually moving the money.** Learn to read it and a lot of daily market noise starts to make sense.

The labels are simpler than they sound. FIIs, foreign institutional investors, now officially called FPIs, are overseas funds putting money into Indian shares. DIIs, domestic institutional investors, are Indian mutual funds, insurers, and pension funds. The exchanges publish what each group bought and sold every single day.

## How to read the daily number

The number is reported as net buying or net selling in rupees crore. **A positive figure means that group was a net buyer that day, a negative one means a net seller, and the two are best read together, not alone.** When FIIs are selling and DIIs are buying by similar amounts, the money is changing hands domestically and prices often hold steady.

The key is to watch the trend, not the day. A single session can be thrown off by one large block deal or an index rebalancing, so a week or a month of data tells you far more than any one evening's print. Our [FII vs DII](/blog/fii-vs-dii) explainer breaks down who these players are and why they so often move in opposite directions.

## What actually drives each side

The two groups answer to different masters, which is why they so often pull in opposite directions. **FIIs are driven by global factors: the US dollar, US interest rates, and how India looks against other emerging markets.** When the dollar is strong and US bonds pay well, foreign money tends to leave riskier markets like India, regardless of how Indian companies are doing. That is why a strong US jobs report can trigger FII selling in Mumbai the next day.

DIIs answer to something far steadier: the monthly savings of ordinary Indians. **Because SIP money arrives every month whether the market is up or down, DII buying is far more predictable than the on-off nature of foreign flows.** That steadiness is exactly what has let domestic institutions absorb waves of foreign selling without the market cracking, and it is the single biggest change in how the Indian market behaves today versus a decade ago.

## What a flip actually signals

The most useful moment is not a big number but a change in direction. Here is how to read the main combinations.

| FII | DII | What it often signals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selling | Buying | Domestic support absorbing foreign exit (India 2025-26) |
| Buying | Buying | Broad conviction, usually a strong up move |
| Selling | Selling | Genuine risk-off, few buyers, prices fall |
| Buying | Selling | Foreign-led rally, domestic profit-taking |

**The signal worth waiting for is a flip: FIIs turning from persistent sellers into buyers, or DII inflows slowing.** A sustained flip changes the balance of the market, whereas a single loud day rarely does.

## Why DIIs changed the game

For years, foreign money set the market's direction and Indian investors followed. That has reversed. **Backed by record monthly SIP inflows of over Rs 26,000 crore, domestic institutions have become the market's shock absorber, buying steadily even as foreigners sell.**

The scale is striking. In May 2026 alone, DIIs bought about Rs 82,668 crore of shares, as our [DII record buying](/blog/dii-sip-record-buying-india-2026) piece details, absorbing an FII exit that would once have crashed the market. The persistent foreign selling is covered in our [FPI outflows](/blog/fpi-outflows-india-2026-record) coverage.

The result is a structural shift. **Domestic investors now own a larger share of the Indian market than foreigners for the first time**, a milestone detailed in our [FPI ownership at a 14-year low](/blog/fpi-ownership-india-14-year-low-2026) piece. It means Indian savings, not global sentiment, increasingly set the floor under the market.

## How to use it without overreading

<Callout type="risk" title="Do not trade on one day">

A single day's FII or DII figure is provisional and easily distorted by one block deal. Treat it as a weather reading, not a forecast. The trend over weeks, and any sustained flip in direction, is the signal. One red day is noise.

</Callout>

Flows also connect to other clues. Heavy FII selling pressures the rupee, since foreigners convert rupees back to dollars, which is why the currency and foreign flows move together. And when one group rotates between sectors rather than the whole market, that shows up too, as our [sector rotation](/blog/sector-rotation-smart-money-india) piece explains.

Read this way, the nightly FII and DII line stops being trivia and becomes a genuine gauge of conviction. It will not tell you what happens tomorrow, but over weeks it shows you who believes in this market, who is leaving, and, most usefully, the moment that balance begins to turn.
