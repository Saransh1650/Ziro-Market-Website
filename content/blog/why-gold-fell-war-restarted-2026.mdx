The textbook says war lifts gold. The first week of September 2026 said otherwise. **Renewed US strikes on Iranian targets near the Strait of Hormuz sent crude sharply higher toward $97 a barrel, and gold fell to about $4,336.30 an ounce, a four-week low, down roughly 7% from $4,670 on 24 August.**

This is not a broken market. It is two chains of causation pulling in opposite directions, and the stronger one won.

<Callout type="note" title="The quick answer">

Gold fell because the war reached it through the inflation channel, not the safe-haven channel. Higher oil raised expected inflation, markets priced the Federal Reserve keeping policy tighter for longer, and real yields and the dollar rose. Gold pays no coupon, so higher real yields mechanically reduce what investors will pay for it. Spot gold was $4,336.30 on 2 September 2026, with silver at $63.87.

</Callout>

## The two chains, and why one wins

Every geopolitical shock reaches gold along two routes at once.

**The safe-haven chain is the famous one.** Conflict raises uncertainty, investors want an asset with no counterparty and no government behind it, and gold rises. This chain is real, and it is what carried gold through most of 2025.

**The rates chain is the quieter and, right now, stronger one.** A war that threatens oil supply raises expected inflation. Higher expected inflation, in a world where the central bank has already signalled it will not tolerate a drift above target, means tighter policy for longer. Tighter policy means higher real yields and a stronger dollar. **Gold pays no coupon, so when the risk-free real return rises, the opportunity cost of holding a metal that yields nothing rises with it, and the price falls.**

In the first days of September, the second chain simply had more force. The market moved from pricing roughly a 35% chance of a September rate hike before Fed chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech to about 66% after it and after the strikes, with the US 10-year Treasury yield reaching 4.78%.

| Channel | What the war did | Effect on gold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Safe haven | Raised geopolitical uncertainty | Positive, but modest |
| Inflation expectations | Pushed crude toward $97 | Negative via rate expectations |
| Real yields | 10-year to 4.78% on hike bets | Strongly negative |
| Dollar | Strengthened on tighter policy | Negative for dollar-priced gold |

## Why Warsh mattered more than the missiles

Because he changed the reaction function. **Fed chair Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole keynote on 28 August 2026 to sharpen his warning on inflation and pledge a return to the 2% target, which told markets that an energy shock would be met with tighter policy rather than tolerance.**

That single shift rewrote how every subsequent headline gets priced. **Before Warsh, an oil spike was a reason to buy gold as an inflation hedge; after Warsh, an oil spike is a reason to expect a rate hike, which is a reason to sell gold.** Same event, opposite trade, because the central bank's likely response changed in between. Our [Jackson Hole 2026](/blog/jackson-hole-2026-warsh-india-impact) coverage tracks what he actually said.

The awkward consequence is that the Fed now looks at odds with the US Treasury, which spent August expanding its long-dated bond buybacks specifically to hold yields down.

## What it means for Indian buyers

Indian gold has fallen, but less than the dollar chart suggests, and that gap is the rupee. **In India 24 carat gold was about Rs 1,55,350 per 10 grams and 22 carat about Rs 1,42,400 (as of 3 September 2026), against Rs 1,63,970 on 25 August**, so roughly a 5% decline in rupees against about 7% in dollars.

Two forces cushion the local price. **Import duty and GST are levied on the landed value, and a weaker rupee raises the rupee cost of the same ounce**, which is why Indian buyers rarely capture the full extent of a dollar-gold fall. The rupee has actually held up better than expected through this episode, supported by Reserve Bank of India dollar sales and strong FCNR(B) inflows.

For anyone buying for a wedding rather than a portfolio, the practical read is simpler. A 5% fall arriving just as the festive and wedding season begins is the first genuine discount of the year, and the daily level sits on our [gold rate today in India](/blog/gold-rate-today-india) page.

## What would turn gold back up

**A Federal Reserve that hikes and signals it is done would do it.** Gold usually troughs when the market stops adding to its expected path of tightening, not when the last hike is delivered.

**An actual supply disruption, rather than a threat, would also do it**, because a closure of the Strait of Hormuz would push oil far enough to make the growth shock dominate the inflation shock, a scenario our [what happens if the Strait of Hormuz closes](/blog/what-happens-if-strait-of-hormuz-closes) explainer sets out.

**Central bank buying remains the slow, structural bid underneath all of this.** Purchases reached 288.9 tonnes in the second quarter of 2026, up 62% year on year, and that demand is policy-driven rather than price-driven, which is why our [gold price forecast](/blog/gold-6000-dollars-2026-forecast) analysis treats it as the floor rather than the trigger.

## Risks to monitor

<Callout type="risk" title="The trap in buying the dip on a headline">

Buying gold because a war escalated, without checking what the escalation does to rate expectations, is how investors got hurt this week. In an inflation-fighting regime, bad news for the world can be bad news for gold, because it makes the central bank more hawkish rather than less.

</Callout>

The second risk is the mirror image. If the Fed hikes on 16 September and the economy weakens quickly afterwards, the rate path reverses, real yields fall, and the same gold that de-rated in September can re-rate sharply.

The third is that oil does the deciding. Gold's next few weeks are more a function of Brent than of any gold-specific factor, which is an uncomfortable position for an asset people buy precisely to avoid depending on other people's decisions. This is general information, not investment advice.

The useful takeaway is not that gold failed. It is that gold hedges currency debasement and financial stress, not geopolitical drama in general, and this month the market was pricing a central bank's response rather than the event itself.
