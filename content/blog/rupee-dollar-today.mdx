The rupee stopped falling, which this month counts as news. **The rupee closed at 95.88 per US dollar on 18 September 2026, marginally stronger than the 95.94 of the previous session, after breaching 96 intraday earlier in the week.** The stabiliser was oil, with Brent slipping back under $100 for the first time since the Hormuz disruption began to bite.

The round trip is still the story of the month. **The currency rallied to a two-month high near 94.30 in early September before oil and US yields reversed it**, and it now sits about 1.1% above the record low of 96.96 set in May 2026.

## Why the rupee is falling

Oil is the first and larger reason. **India imports more than 90% of the crude it uses, so Brent running from about $92 in late August to roughly $107 by mid-September directly widened the dollar bill India must fund**, a transmission our [crude oil price today](/blog/crude-oil-price-today) page tracks. That pressure eased when Brent fell back to $99.29 on 19 September 2026, the first close under $100 since the disruption began.

The second is the dollar itself. **The Federal Reserve raised rates to 3.75% to 4.00% on 16 September 2026 after US CPI came in at 3.4%, and projected a policy rate near 4.1% through 2027**, as our [US CPI August 2026](/blog/us-cpi-august-2026-fed-hike-india) piece explains. Higher US rates raise the return on dollar assets and pull capital away from emerging markets.

The third is flows. **Foreign portfolio investors have pulled roughly Rs 2,37,353 crore out of Indian equities in 2026**, and every rupee of that has to be converted into dollars on the way out, covered in our [FPI outflows](/blog/fpi-outflows-india-2026-record) analysis. They bought Rs 599.54 crore on 18 September, a single session that does not change the year.

## How the RBI is defending it

Not with rates, with reserves. **The Reserve Bank of India sold at least $8 billion in a single week in early September, with some estimates closer to $15 billion, and has drawn $136.37 billion through its FCNR(B) swap window**, a mechanism our [FCNR swap](/blog/rbi-fcnr-swap-136-billion-rupee-defence-2026) piece explains.

The firepower is real. **India's foreign exchange reserves stand at a record $740.8 billion**, which is why the rupee has fallen in an orderly way rather than gapping. Intervention slows a move, it does not remove the cause.

## What a weaker rupee does to you

| Who | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Importers, airlines, electronics buyers | Higher input and landed costs |
| Students and travellers abroad | Every dollar of fees or spending costs more |
| IT services, pharma and textile exporters | Dollar revenue converts into more rupees |
| Households | Imported inflation through fuel and goods |
| RBI | Less room to cut the 5.25% repo rate |

**The cruel part of an oil-driven depreciation is that it raises prices at exactly the moment the central bank would otherwise want to support growth.**

## What to watch

**The first is whether Brent holds under $100.** Oil falling back at source does more for the rupee than any amount of intervention, because it shrinks the dollar bill rather than financing it.

**The second is the new US sanctions law signed on 18 September 2026**, which authorises tariffs of up to 100% on the largest buyers of Russian energy and names India among them. A tariff would hit exports and the currency at the same time.

**The third is India's inflation path after August CPI at 4.82%**, which decides whether the Reserve Bank of India retains any theoretical room to ease later in the year.

**The fourth is the record low at 96.96.** A break below that level would be the psychological marker that draws headlines and can accelerate corporate hedging, which in turn accelerates the move.

## Risks to monitor

<Callout type="risk" title="Reserves buy time, not direction">

A record $740.8 billion of reserves lets the RBI control the pace of a depreciation, not its direction. While US rates are rising, the underlying dollar demand keeps building, and intervention spends reserves to slow something it cannot stop. Cheaper oil is the only thing that shrinks the bill itself.

</Callout>

The second risk is the feedback loop. **A weaker rupee raises imported inflation, which raises the cost of the RBI holding rates, which further discourages foreign inflows.**

The third is the upside case. **Any Gulf de-escalation reverses the oil premium, and the rupee's recent two-month high near 94.30 shows how quickly it recovers when the pressure lifts.** This is general information, not investment advice.

The rupee was not sold this month because of anything that happened in India. It is the price at which a country that imports nine barrels in ten settles its bills with the rest of the world, and for the first time since August that bill has started coming down.
