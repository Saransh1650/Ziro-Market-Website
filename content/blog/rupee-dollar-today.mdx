## What Is Happening

The rupee is grinding toward new lows. **At about 95.44 to the dollar, it is close to its weakest level ever**, having traded in a roughly 94.31 to 95.44 band over the past week. The currency is being pulled in two directions, and the downward forces are winning.

On the supportive side is oil. As the US-Iran ceasefire reopened the Strait of Hormuz, crude has fallen about 40% from its 2026 peak above $110, with Brent now near $72. **Because India imports more than 85% of its oil in dollars, falling crude cuts the country's import bill and the demand for dollars**, which would normally lift the rupee. You can track the crude side on our [crude oil price today](/blog/crude-oil-price-today) page.

Pulling the other way is a wall of dollar demand. A firm US dollar globally, persistent foreign selling of Indian equities, and steady buying from foreign banks and oil companies have overwhelmed the oil tailwind. **The Reserve Bank of India has intervened in both the spot and offshore forwards markets, but it has only slowed the slide, not reversed it.**

## Why This Matters

The rupee's level touches almost everyone. **A weaker rupee makes imported inflation stickier**, feeding into the cost of fuel, electronics, and travel across the economy. Even with oil cheap, a currency near record lows keeps the landed cost of imports high, blunting some of the benefit of the crude crash.

It cuts both ways for different groups. Importers and consumers feel the pinch when the rupee weakens, while exporters and dollar earners, including IT services firms and NRIs remitting money home, get more rupees per dollar. A rupee near 95.44 is a headache for one side and a windfall for the other.

For the broader economy, a rupee this weak despite falling oil is a warning that the pressure is structural, not just about crude. Foreign outflows and a strong dollar are harder to fix than an oil spike, which is why the RBI keeps intervening rather than letting the currency find its own level.

## What To Watch

The first thing to watch is foreign portfolio flows. **Since sustained equity outflows are a big part of the rupee's weakness, a turn back to buying by foreign investors would do more to lift the rupee than cheap oil alone.** The flow data is the currency's best leading indicator right now.

The second is the US dollar and the Fed. A strong dollar globally pressures all emerging-market currencies, so any further hawkish shift from the Fed could push the rupee to fresh lows even with oil cheap.

The third is oil. The crude crash is the rupee's main cushion, so any rebound in prices, especially a flare-up around the Strait of Hormuz, would remove that support and likely accelerate the fall.

## Risks To Monitor

The clearest risk is heavier foreign outflows. If selling in Indian equities picks up, the rupee could slide past its record low regardless of the oil tailwind.

A second risk is a stronger dollar. If the Fed leans more hawkish and the dollar rallies further, the rupee could weaken again even as the RBI leans against it.

The third is an oil rebound. The ceasefire is a framework, not a settled peace, and a return of conflict could spike crude and knock away the rupee's main support. This is general information, not investment advice.

At about 95.44, the rupee is testing the limits of what cheap oil and RBI intervention can hold. Its next move depends less on anything happening in Mumbai or Delhi than on whether foreign money keeps leaving and how far the dollar runs.
