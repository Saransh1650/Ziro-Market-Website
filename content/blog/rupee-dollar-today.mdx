The rupee spent the first week of September recovering and the second giving it all back. **The rupee breached 96 per US dollar on 17 September 2026, the first time since 24 July, after the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate by 25 basis points to 3.75% to 4.00% and the US 10-year Treasury yield held near its highest level since 2007.**

The round trip is the story. **The currency had rallied to a two-month high near 94.30 in early September before oil and US yields reversed it**, and it now sits about 1.5% above the record low of 96.96 set in May 2026.

## Why the rupee is falling

Oil is the first and larger reason. **India imports more than 90% of the crude it uses, so Brent back near $107 on 15 September after the Oman talks were postponed directly widens the dollar bill India must fund**, a transmission our [crude oil price today](/blog/crude-oil-price-today) page tracks. Houthi strikes on Saudi energy facilities and a 1.9 million barrel a day drop in Saudi output in August added to the Strait of Hormuz disruption.

<Logo src="/images/photos/indian-rupee-banknote.jpg" alt="An Indian rupee banknote, the currency that breached 96 per US dollar in September 2026" width={680} caption="The rupee breached 96 per dollar intraday on 17 September 2026 before closing at 95.94, its weakest close since 24 July." credit="Photo: Ravi Dwivedi / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0" />

The second is the dollar itself. **US CPI came in at 3.4% on 11 September and producer prices at 5.4% a day earlier, pushing market-implied odds of a Federal Reserve hike on 16 September to roughly 90%**, as our [US CPI August 2026](/blog/us-cpi-august-2026-fed-hike-india) piece explains. Higher US rates raise the return on dollar assets and pull capital away from emerging markets.

The third is flows. **Foreign portfolio investors have pulled roughly Rs 2,37,353 crore out of Indian equities in 2026**, and every rupee of that has to be converted into dollars on the way out, covered in our [FPI outflows](/blog/fpi-outflows-india-2026-record) analysis.

## How the RBI is defending it

Not with rates, with reserves. **The Reserve Bank of India sold at least $8 billion in a single week in early September, with some estimates closer to $15 billion, and has drawn $136.37 billion through its FCNR(B) swap window**, a mechanism our [FCNR swap](/blog/rbi-fcnr-swap-136-billion-rupee-defence-2026) piece explains.

The firepower is real. **India's foreign exchange reserves stand at a record $740.8 billion**, which is why the rupee has fallen in an orderly way rather than gapping. Intervention slows a move, it does not remove the cause.

## What a weaker rupee does to you

| Who | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Importers, airlines, electronics buyers | Higher input and landed costs |
| Students and travellers abroad | Every dollar of fees or spending costs more |
| IT services, pharma and textile exporters | Dollar revenue converts into more rupees |
| Households | Imported inflation through fuel and goods |
| RBI | Less room to cut the 5.25% repo rate |

**The cruel part of an oil-driven depreciation is that it raises prices at exactly the moment the central bank would otherwise want to support growth.**

## What to watch

**The first is the Federal Reserve on 16 September.** A hike is nearly fully priced, so the rupee's reaction depends on whether the statement signals further tightening.

**The second is oil.** Brent falling back under $95 would do more for the rupee than any amount of intervention, since it shrinks the import bill at source.

**The third is India's own CPI on 12 September**, which decides whether the Reserve Bank of India retains any theoretical room to ease later in the year.

**The fourth is the record low at 96.96.** A break below that level would be the psychological marker that draws headlines and can accelerate corporate hedging, which in turn accelerates the move.

## Risks to monitor

<Callout type="risk" title="Reserves buy time, not direction">

A record $740.8 billion of reserves lets the RBI control the pace of a depreciation, not its direction. As long as crude stays above $100 and US rates rise, the underlying dollar demand keeps building, and intervention spends reserves to slow something it cannot stop.

</Callout>

The second risk is the feedback loop. **A weaker rupee raises imported inflation, which raises the cost of the RBI holding rates, which further discourages foreign inflows.**

The third is the upside case. **Any Gulf de-escalation reverses the oil premium, and the rupee's recent two-month high near 94.30 shows how quickly it recovers when the pressure lifts.** This is general information, not investment advice.

The rupee is not being sold because of anything that happened in India this week. It is the price at which a country that imports nine barrels in ten settles its bills with the rest of the world, and that bill went up.
