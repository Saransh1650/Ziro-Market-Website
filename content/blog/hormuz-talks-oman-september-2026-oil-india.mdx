The first real chance of de-escalation in this oil crisis lasted three days, from announcement to cancellation.

**The meeting between Iran, Gulf states and Iraq scheduled for 14 September 2026 in Salalah, Oman was postponed indefinitely, with Oman saying the appropriate conditions were not in place, and Brent crude climbed back to about $107 a barrel by 15 September after settling near $104 when the talks were announced.**

The reasons are specific rather than atmospheric. **Saudi Arabia objected to proposed amendments to the existing Iran-Oman corridor arrangement, and Bahrain refused outright to meet Iranian officials, citing repeated missile and drone attacks on its territory.**

Oman postponed the 14 September 2026 Salalah meeting between Iran, Gulf states and Iraq on Hormuz shipping, saying conditions were not right. Saudi Arabia objected to amendments to the Iran-Oman corridor plan and Bahrain declined to attend. No new date has been set. Brent crude returned to about $107 a barrel, having settled near $104 when the meeting was announced.

## What was on the table, and why it stalled

The plan was logistics, not peace. **Iran and Oman had negotiated a temporary corridor under which ships would enter the strait through Iranian waters and mostly exit through Omani territory, with mine clearance and short-term management of the waterway**, and they agreed a bilateral version in late August 2026.

Salalah was meant to widen that into something regional, which is where it broke. **A bilateral arrangement between Iran and Oman is a shipping deal. A regional one requires Gulf states to sit at a table with Tehran while missiles are still landing**, and Bahrain's refusal made that explicit.

There is a second objection underneath the first. **Saudi Arabia and Qatar were willing to attend but wary of appearing to legitimise Tehran's position on the strait**, because any negotiated corridor implicitly concedes that Iran has a say over who passes.

<Pullquote cite="The structural problem">

A corridor deal asks the Gulf to accept Iranian permission as the basis for Gulf shipping.

</Pullquote>

## How the oil price handled it

Fast, and in both directions. **Brent settled near $104 on 11 September when the meeting was announced, then returned to about $107 by 15 September once it collapsed**, which tells you how thin the de-escalation premium was.

The wider trend has not changed. **Brent is up roughly 19% over a month, the International Energy Agency calls the Hormuz disruption the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market, and the war now has a second chokepoint** after Houthi forces took Yemen's Red Sea coastline, covered in our [two chokepoints](/blog/two-chokepoints-hormuz-bab-el-mandeb-india-2026) analysis.

## What it costs India

The transmission is arithmetic. **India imports more than 90% of the crude it uses, spent $134.7 billion on crude in FY26, and every $10 on the barrel is estimated to cost 20 to 30 basis points of GDP growth.**

It is already visible in the data. **India's CPI inflation rose to 4.82% in August 2026, a seven-month high, with food at 5.95%**, and while vegetables did most of that damage, fuel and transport costs are the layer underneath, as our [India CPI August 2026](/blog/india-cpi-august-2026-inflation-rbi) piece sets out.

## What investors should watch

**The first is whether a new date is set at all.** Iran's foreign minister said Tehran remains committed to regional consultations and would coordinate with Oman on a new date. A date being announced is the signal, not the statements around it.

**The second is whether the bilateral Iran-Oman corridor keeps operating.** The regional version stalled, but the bilateral arrangement from late August was not cancelled, and tanker movements through it matter more than any communique.

**The third is Bahrain and Saudi Arabia.** The objections that killed this meeting are about legitimacy rather than logistics, and they do not resolve because oil is expensive. They resolve when the attacks stop.

**The fourth is the Federal Reserve on 16 September**, because an oil shock that persists alongside a hawkish Fed is the combination that has driven the rupee and Indian equities all quarter, set out in our [US CPI August 2026](/blog/us-cpi-august-2026-fed-hike-india) analysis.

## Risks to monitor

<Callout type="risk" title="Diplomacy is now a trading event, not a trend">

This market has learned to buy every de-escalation headline and sell every collapse, and both moves have been worth several dollars a barrel within days. Treating any single meeting as the end of the shock has been the most expensive assumption of the year, because the shipping problem is downstream of a war that none of these meetings is trying to end.

</Callout>

The second risk is escalation rather than stalemate. **Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman asked the US President twice on 10 September for strikes on the Houthi movement and was refused**, with intelligence and targeting support offered instead, which leaves the Red Sea problem unresolved on its own track.

The third is the demand side. **The IEA has cut its global oil demand outlook and the EIA raised its 2027 US production forecast to 14.3 million barrels a day**, so the medium-term balance is softer than this quarter's price suggests. This is general information, not investment advice.

For three days the oil market had a story about how this ends. It turned out to be a story about how hard it is to get eight governments into one room in Salalah, which is roughly where the price is now trading.
